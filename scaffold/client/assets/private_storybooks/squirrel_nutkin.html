The Tale of Squirrel Nutkin

Once upon a time, on the shore of a big, still lake, there lived a family of red squirrels, and among them was the cheekiest, chatteriest one of the bunch, a squirrel named Nutkin.

In the middle of the lake sat a small island covered in oak trees, thick with acorns every autumn, and in a hollow at the top of the tallest oak lived an old owl named Old Brown, who liked peace and quiet above almost everything else.

When the nuts turned ripe, Nutkin and his many cousins built little rafts out of twigs and sailed across the water to gather them. But since the island belonged to Old Brown, it was only right to ask permission first. So each morning, the squirrels climbed up near his doorway, set down a small gift like a shiny beetle or a fat mouse, bowed politely, and asked, "Please, Old Brown, will you give us leave to gather nuts today?"

Old Brown never said a word back. He would blink his enormous yellow eyes once, slow as anything, and the squirrels took that to mean yes. Off they'd scurry to fill their pouches with acorns.

Every squirrel was polite and well-mannered about the whole business. Every squirrel, that is, except Nutkin.

Instead of bowing, Nutkin liked to dance. Instead of a gift, he brought riddles, and he would hop right up close to Old Brown and sing out things like, "I have a house of leaves up high, and never am I hungry, and never am I fed -- now what could I be, Old Brown, if you're not too sleepy-headed?"

Old Brown never answered a single riddle. He only sat there, blinking slowly, saying nothing at all, no matter how close Nutkin hopped or how loudly he sang.

Day after day, this went on. The other squirrels gathered their acorns quietly and paddled home well before dark, but Nutkin grew bolder and bolder. He tickled Old Brown's feathers with a bit of nettle. He tossed little pebbles down from the branches above. He sang his riddles louder and louder, until even his own cousins whispered that he really ought to stop before he landed himself in trouble.

Nutkin didn't listen, of course.

On the very last day of the nut season, he got so carried away with his teasing that he hopped right up onto Old Brown's own doorstep and shouted his silliest, loudest riddle yet, directly in the old owl's face.

That was the last straw. Old Brown's eyes snapped wide open, and in one great swoop, he lunged forward and caught hold of Nutkin's long, bushy tail.

Nutkin had never in his life been so frightened. He squealed and thrashed and pulled with all his might, and just as Old Brown opened his beak to give him the stern scolding he had certainly earned, Nutkin wriggled free and tumbled down through the branches into the bushes below, leaving nothing behind but a fright he would remember for a very long time.

He didn't stop running until he'd scrambled onto his raft and paddled all the way back across the lake, his little heart pounding the whole way home.

From that day on, Nutkin still chattered plenty, because that was simply who he was, but he never once went back to Old Brown's island again. And whenever his cousins rowed over to ask permission to gather nuts, they say Nutkin waited quietly on the shore, bowing very respectfully, from a very safe and sensible distance.
