The Tale of Jemima Puddle-Duck

Once there was a duck named Jemima Puddle-Duck who lived on a farm and was quite unhappy about one particular thing: every time she tried to sit on her own eggs to hatch them, the farmer's wife came along and took them away to give to a hen instead, because the farmer's wife didn't trust a scatterbrained duck like Jemima to manage the job herself.

Jemima thought this was terribly unfair. So one fine spring afternoon, wearing her best shawl and bonnet, she decided she would find a quiet, hidden spot to lay her eggs where nobody would ever think to look for them.

She waddled off down the road, and when walking grew tiresome, she spread her wings and flew the rest of the way, over hedges and fields, until she landed in a shady wood on top of a hill.

There, sitting on a stump and dressed very smartly in a fine, sandy-colored coat, was a fox, who rose at once and bowed as if she were the finest lady he'd ever met.

"Good afternoon," he said, in a voice as smooth as syrup. "You look like you're searching for something, my dear madam. Might I be of some assistance?"

Jemima, who was flattered by his fancy manners and didn't know very much about foxes, told him her whole trouble.

"How very sad," said the fox, his eyes glinting in a way a wiser duck might have noticed. "As it happens, I have a cozy little shed just through those trees, piled with soft feathers. You'd be perfectly private there. Come and see for yourself."

Jemima followed him to a strange, dim little shed tucked deep in the woods, and it was indeed soft and quiet, so she settled right in and began to lay her eggs, quite pleased with her clever discovery.

Over the following days, she visited her secret nest each afternoon, and the fox was always there, always polished and charming, always asking her odd little favors: would she mind fetching some sage from the garden, and thyme, and onions too, for what he called "a very special stuffing." Jemima, eager to be a good guest, trotted off each time to gather his herbs, never once wondering why a fox would want a stuffing at all.

Now, the farm's old sheepdog, a sensible collie named Kep, had noticed Jemima flying off toward the woods every single afternoon with a basket, and he thought it looked odd. So one day he followed her quietly and peeked through a gap in the shed wall.

Kep didn't like what he saw one bit. This was no ordinary shed -- it was the fox's own den, and something about the fox's smooth manner and that shut-up little room made every hair on Kep's back stand up. Every instinct he had told him this was no place for a duck to be spending her afternoons. Kep understood at once that Jemima might be in real danger, even though she hadn't the faintest idea of it herself.

He didn't waste a single moment. He raced back to the farm and fetched two big, bouncing foxhound puppies, and the three of them tore back through the woods together, barking as loudly as they possibly could.

The fox took one look at two galloping foxhounds bursting toward his door and didn't stay around to argue. He leapt straight out the back window and streaked off into the trees so fast his tail practically whistled behind him, and nobody on that farm ever saw him again.

Jemima, startled but perfectly unharmed, was gently gathered up and walked all the way home by Kep, quacking the entire time about what a fright she'd had, and admitting that perhaps foxes weren't quite as gentlemanly as they seemed.

In all the commotion, her eggs sadly never got the chance to hatch, but Jemima didn't mind too terribly, because that summer, with the farmer's wife keeping a closer and kinder eye on her, she finally got to sit on a nest of her very own. In time, she hatched a whole family of soft, fluffy ducklings, and from then on, she never again wandered off to visit strange foxes in fine waistcoats.
