# Who to call and what to say

> **DRAFT, not reviewed, not medical or legal advice.**
> Status: DRAFT_UNREVIEWED, revision 4. Researched 2026-11-14. Needs review by a qualified paediatric reviewer before it appears in the app. Covers children from about 1 year to puberty (infant steps are not covered). Numbers in [brackets] are the source list at the bottom.

## 1. If it looks serious: call emergency services now

US 911, UK 999 (112 also works), Canada 911, Australia 000, EU 112. Call for any of these:

- Breathing has stopped or is laboured, or the child has trouble speaking. [0][20]
- Lips or skin turn blue, purple or grey, or look pale or blotchy. [0][20]
- The child cannot be roused, is limp and floppy, or seems unaware of what is going on; or alertness is dropping, or behaviour is strange or withdrawn. [0][20]
- Any seizure (rhythmic jerking with loss of responsiveness). [0][3][20]
- Bleeding you cannot control, or a deep or gaping wound or burn. [0][20]
- Choking on something you cannot dislodge. [3][20]
- A serious allergic reaction. [3][4]
- A head injury followed by lower alertness, confusion, headache, vomiting, irritability or trouble walking. [0]
- Suspected neck, back or pelvis injury: call and keep the child still. [20]
- A child pulled out of water who does not respond. [3]
- Fever together with abnormal appearance, breathing or circulation; or a stiff neck with headache and fever; or a purple or red rash that is spreading fast. [0]
- Vomiting blood, significant blood in stool, or signs of significant dehydration (sunken eyes, no tears or urine, lethargy). [0]
- Severe or increasing pain anywhere, or a heat or cold injury such as heat exhaustion or frostbite. [0]
- A child who has swallowed poison or too much medicine and has collapsed, is seizing, is struggling to breathe or cannot be woken. UK: 999 for any swallowed poison or overdose. [10][20][21][3]
- Burn emergencies: a burn that is large (about the size of the child's palm or bigger) or deep; a burn on the face, hands, genitals or bottom; an electrical or chemical burn; skin that looks charred, leathery, white, brown or black, or has no feeling; breathing trouble, smoke or fume exposure, confusion or difficulty waking; or you are not sure how serious it is. [6][7][18][43][44]
- More than one child hurt or seriously ill at the same time. [0]
- You are not sure what is wrong but believe the child is in danger. The operator will guide you. [3]

## 3. What to say on the call

1. Where you are: the exact address, nearest cross street or landmark. Mobile calls may not be located automatically, so be ready to say it. [0][1][14][32]
2. What happened and the main problem (for example: not breathing, seizure, bleeding). [14][32]
3. The child's age, whether the child is awake and whether the child is breathing; the operator may also ask about medical history. [1][32]
4. A phone number they can call you back on (and if a mobile call drops, give your number). [1][14]
5. Stay on the line until the rescue crew arrives, unless the operator tells you that you can hang up. They may coach you through first aid or CPR or tell you to move somewhere safer. [1][32][14]
6. Make a voice call rather than texting if you can safely do so (in Australia you cannot text 000 at all). Put the phone on speaker so your hands are free. [1][27][32]
7. Be patient and answer the questions calmly, slowly and clearly; the questions let responders send the right kind of help. [1][32]
8. Do not hang up, even if you think the call was a mistake. [1]
9. While waiting: stay with the child, turn on lights (inside and outside) and unlock the front door so responders can get in, open curtains, lock pets away (hazard lights if in a car), and call again if the child's condition or your location changes. [14][32]
10. For the crew: tell them about known allergies, and if it was given, the time any emergency medicine such as epinephrine was given. [14][4]

## 4. Poison line (not always 911)

- Child may have swallowed, breathed in, or got on skin or in eyes a medicine, chemical or other possible poison, even if the child looks fine. US: call Poison Help, 1-800-222-1222. It is free, confidential and open 24 hours a day, and it connects to your regional poison centre. [3][9][38]
- If the child is unwell, or you believe the child is in danger, call 911 rather than waiting for the poison line. [3]
- poison.org offers a free online tool, but call the phone line for pregnancy, babies under 6 months, adults over 79, several substances at once, or self-harm. [37]
- Do not wait for symptoms before getting poison advice. Australia: call the Poisons Information Line; call 000 if someone has stopped breathing, collapsed, had a seizure, had a severe allergic reaction or has been exposed to a harmful substance. Canada: get advice from a poison information centre or call 911 right away; if the child shows any signs of illness, altered awareness or anything else that makes you immediately concerned, call 911 or your local ambulance service. [31][35]
- If the child has collapsed, is having a seizure, has trouble breathing or cannot be woken, call 911 (or the local emergency number) instead of the poison line. [10]
- Have the product, container or medicine package in hand when you call. [3]
- Do not try to make the child vomit. [31][35][21]
- UK: if you think a child has swallowed, touched or breathed in something harmful, call 999 or go to A&E (do not drive yourself). If you are not sure whether it is harmful, call NHS 111. [21]
- UK: a swallowed button battery or magnet means 999 or A&E straight away. [20]

## 5. Doctor, nurse line, urgent care or ER

**Doctor or nurse line**

- Not an emergency and you are unsure where to go: call the child's own paediatrician or doctor, including after hours (many offices give advice by phone). [2]
- Problems suitable for a call to the paediatrician first: minor burns, ear pain, pink eye, possible urinary infection, sore throat, rashes, vomiting or diarrhoea with mild dehydration, simple wounds, objects stuck in ear or nose, colds, coughs and allergies. Minor means minor: a serious burn or severe allergic reaction is an emergency. [2]
- A rough test: if the child can walk, talk, interact and play, it is probably not an emergency (still ask if unsure). [2]
- Needs prompt same-day medical advice but not an ambulance: fever in a child who looks more than mildly ill, severe vomiting or diarrhoea, a cut that may need stitches, animal bites that break the skin. [0]
- UK: NHS 111 is for urgent problems that are not emergencies; for a child under 5 use the phone, not the online service. [15]
- Australia: healthdirect nurse line 1800 022 222 (registered nurse, 24 hours). [33]

**Urgent care or ER**

- Go to the emergency room, or call 911 for an ambulance, for severe injury or life-threatening illness. An ambulance is safer than driving a seriously ill or injured child yourself. [2]
- Emergency room list: wounds that keep bleeding, a seizure lasting more than 2 minutes in a child who has never had one (call emergency services for any seizure), head injury with changed behaviour, loss of consciousness, severe belly pain, severe burns, swallowed object with trouble breathing or swallowing, severe fractures, trouble breathing, vomiting or coughing blood, severe neck stiffness or rash with fever, poisoning, worsening asthma or diabetes. [2]
- A baby under 60 days (about 2 months) with a fever above 100.4 F needs emergency care now. (Infants are outside this guide's scope.) [2][0]
- Burns: call emergency services for a burn that is large (about the size of the child's palm or bigger) or deep; a burn on the face, hands, genitals or bottom; an electrical or chemical burn; skin that looks charred, leathery, white, brown or black, or has no feeling; breathing trouble, smoke or fume exposure, confusion or difficulty waking; or you are not sure how serious it is. A blistering or swollen burn bigger than the child's hand, or any burn on a foot or over a joint, should be seen immediately by a paediatrician or in an emergency room. [6][18][43][44][7]
- UK: go to A&E for a cut where bleeding will not stop with pressure, spurting blood, an embedded object, or a very large or deep wound; do not drive yourself if you are the injured person (ask someone else or call 999). [19]
- UK: a suspected broken arm or leg, if the child cannot use the limb, goes to A&E. If you cannot move the child without pain, call 999. [20]

## 6. Have this ready (and hand it to a sitter or grandparent)

- Your street address, posted by the home phone. [0]
- The child's paediatrician or GP name and contact details. [3][14]
- A list of the child's regular medicines (and bring the medicines with the child to hospital). [3][14]
- Known allergies (to tell the paramedics). [14]
- For a suspected poisoning: the product, medicine bottle or packet, what it was, how much, when and how it happened (swallowed, breathed in, skin or eye), and what symptoms there are. [3][21][31]
- Any care plan the child has (some conditions in a care plan require the parent to be told). [0]

## 7. Universal first steps

1. Check it is safe for you to approach before you go to the child. Do not move an injured child unless staying put is dangerous (for example fire). [27][3]
2. Check if the child responds: call out their name and tap the shoulder. Do not shake. Shout for help. [12][23][27]
3. If there is no response or it is a serious emergency, have someone call the emergency number straight away, or call yourself with the phone on speaker. [27][25][23][42]
4. Open the airway and look, listen and feel for normal breathing for no more than about 10 seconds; gasping is not normal breathing. Airway: lift the chin and tilt the head back, UNLESS a neck or back injury is possible; then lift the jaw forward without tilting the head (see step 9). [27][25][23][12][28]
5. Child not responding but breathing normally, and no neck or back injury is suspected: place them in the recovery position (on their side, head tilted back), call emergency services, stay with them and keep checking breathing. If a neck or back injury is suspected, do not roll them: see step 9. [22][26][24][27][28]
6. Child not responding and not breathing normally: start CPR and send someone for a defibrillator (AED) if there is one. Cycle of 30 chest compressions then 2 breaths; push on the centre of the chest about 5 cm (2 inches) deep, 100 to 120 per minute, let the chest rise fully. In the US start with the 30 compressions; in the UK start with 5 rescue breaths first. Do not check for a pulse. Continue until help takes over or the child breathes normally. [12][25][23][39][40]
7. If you cannot or will not give breaths, keep giving chest compressions. [23]
8. If you are alone with a child who is not responding and not breathing normally: if you have a phone, put it on speaker and call emergency services NOW, then start CPR and follow the operator. If you have NO phone, do about 1 minute of CPR and then go to call. Take a small child with you if you can (unless a neck or back injury is suspected). If anyone else is there, they call straight away while you start CPR. [39][27][40][25][23][12][42]
9. Suspected neck, back or pelvis injury: call emergency services. Do not move the child and do not tell them to move unless they are in immediate danger. Kneel at their head and hold it with both hands so head, neck and back stay in line. If they are not responding, open the airway by lifting the jaw forward without tilting the head. If they are not breathing, start CPR at once. If they are not responding but are breathing, keep holding the head steady, keep checking their breathing and wait for help; only if you cannot keep the airway open, use the spinal recovery position. If you must move them, two people should move the child so the head and neck do not twist. [28][20][12]
10. Severe bleeding: press firmly on the wound with a clean cloth and keep pressing; shout for someone to call emergency services, or if you are alone put the phone on speaker and call while you keep pressing. If blood soaks through, put more cloth on top and keep pressing. If something is stuck in the wound, leave it and press firmly on either side of it. [19][20][27]
11. Stay with the child, stay as calm as you can (children copy you), and keep the phone line open until told otherwise. [3][1][14]

## 8. Quick recognition (full guides to follow separately)

**Allergic reaction (anaphylaxis)**

Emergency signs:

- Trouble breathing or swallowing, wheezing, chest or throat tightness, hoarse voice
- Swelling of lips, tongue or face
- Hives or rash together with any breathing, dizziness or stomach symptoms
- Pale, blue or grey skin or lips, weak pulse, dizziness, fainting
- Vomiting or diarrhoea, confusion or unusual drowsiness
- In babies: unusual fussiness, sudden drooling, unusual sleepiness

First step: If the child has their own prescribed epinephrine auto-injector, use it straight away, following the device instructions, and then call emergency services at once. If there is no epinephrine, call emergency services at once. Ask for an ambulance, say you think it is anaphylaxis, and say whether epinephrine was given and when. Never use an antihistamine, inhaler or other medicine in place of epinephrine. Keep the child lying down and do not let them stand or walk; if they vomit, turn them onto their side; if they struggle to breathe, tell the call-taker and follow their instructions on position. If symptoms get worse or do not improve within 5 minutes and the child has a second device, a second dose is used, following the child's own plan. Everyone goes to the ER, even if they look better. Dose and device instructions are on the child's own device or plan and are not given here. [4][16][13]

**Seizure**

Emergency signs:

- Any seizure: call emergency services
- Extra warning signs: first ever seizure; lasts longer than 5 minutes; seizures one after another without recovery between them
- Child does not wake up afterwards or has trouble breathing
- Child was injured, or the seizure happened in water
- Child has diabetes or another serious medical condition

First step: Call emergency services (any seizure). Stay with the child and note the time. Do not hold them down and do not put anything in their mouth. Move hard or sharp things away and cushion the head. Lower them to the ground and, unless a neck or back injury is suspected, onto their side. When the jerking stops, open the airway and check breathing; if they are breathing, use the recovery position, unless a neck or back injury is suspected (then keep holding the head steady and follow step 9). [0][20][3][5][17][29][36][28]

**Burn**

Emergency signs:

- Large (about the size of the child's palm or bigger) or deep burn
- Face, hands, genitals or bottom
- Electrical or chemical burn; skin that looks charred, leathery, white, brown or black, or has no feeling
- Breathing trouble, smoke or fume exposure, confusion or difficulty waking; or you are not sure how serious it is
- Blistering burn bigger than the child's hand, or a burn on a foot or over a joint: seen immediately by a paediatrician or in an emergency room

First step: If the burn has any of the call-now signs: call emergency services first (put the phone on speaker, or ask someone else to call), then cool the burn while you wait and while on the call. For every burn, cool at once under cool (not icy) running water for 20 minutes. For a burn on the face, apply a cold wet washcloth right away instead. Do not delay cooling to undress the child; remove clothing or jewellery near the burn only if it is not stuck to the skin. Make sure the child does not get too cold: use a coat or blanket to keep them warm if necessary. No ice, butter, creams or oils. After cooling, cover loosely with cling film laid over the burn (not wrapped; not on the face) or a clean, non-fluffy cloth. [6][7][18][20][43][44]

**Severe bleeding**

Emergency signs:

- Bleeding that will not stop with firm pressure
- Blood spurting or hard to control
- Deep or large wound, or an object stuck in the wound
- Numbness or inability to move near the wound

First step: Press firmly on the wound with a clean cloth or dressing at once and keep pressing. Shout for someone to call emergency services; if you are alone, put the phone on speaker and call while you keep pressing. If blood soaks through, put more cloth on top and keep pressing. If something is stuck in the wound, leave it and press firmly on either side of it. [19][20][27][0]

## 9. Consent (not legal advice)

Not legal advice. Rules on who can agree to a child's treatment vary by state and by country, and emergency consent rules are not covered here. If you believe a child is in danger, call emergency services; the operator will guide you. [8][3]

## 11. Not verified (left out on purpose)

- HRSA poisonhelp.hrsa.gov, the original American Heart Association pages (cpr.heart.org) and the CRTC 911 page (crtc.gc.ca) returned HTTP 403 or a security check and were not opened. AHA content here comes from an AHA summary sheet copied onto a university medical-centre site (source 39); confirm against the AHA original.
- Canadian emergency 911 is supported only by SickKids (hospital site). A search-result snippet (not opened) suggests some remote Canadian areas lack 911; this is NOT stated in the guide. Canada poison line hours and the 811 nurse line were not confirmed on an opened official page.
- A US nurse advice line number (no single national number was found in opened sources).
- What to tell a dispatcher about 'what has already been done': no opened source states this explicitly.
- Child's weight, insurance information, and named emergency contacts as items for a sitter or grandparent handover: no opened authoritative source covers it.
- What to do if you cannot reach a parent: no opened source covers it beyond the AAP advice to phone the child's own paediatrician, even out of hours.
- Emergency medical consent law for non-parent caregivers: not covered by opened sources; varies by place. The 'leave written permission' habit is general practice, not sourced.
- What an urgent care clinic can and cannot treat for children (US); only the AAP's pointer to another article.
- Whether a second adult should call while another gives epinephrine: not stated by any opened source.
- US wording for a swallowed button battery or magnet: AAP lists them under Poison Control, NHS says 999/A&E straight away. A US emergency source was not found; the guide carries the NHS reading for the UK only.
- Infant (under 1 year) CPR, choking and compression technique: out of scope, not researched. Choking first aid while waiting is also out of scope here.
- Epinephrine nasal spray by weight: AAP source 4 says sprays are approved for children weighing 33 pounds or more; no dose or device guidance is given here.
- The NHS 'when to call 999' page does not itself say to stay on the line (it says to follow the call handler); that rule rests on the AAP and healthdirect pages. That NHS page showed a review date of February 2023 with the next review due February 2026, so it was overdue when retrieved; the same location and callback items appear on the AAP and healthdirect pages.
- No opened page says what to do when the US poison line does not connect and the child looks well. The 911 line rests on the AAP rule to call 911 when you believe a child is in danger. HRSA's page could not be opened. Clinician question.
- The order of calling and cooling for a large burn: only healthdirect's 'while waiting for an ambulance' heading implies call, then cool. MedlinePlus says not to put a severe burn in cold water. No source gives an explicit lone-adult order.
- Severe bleeding for a lone adult: St John Ambulance says press first and call if you have not already; NHS says call if the bleeding is severe. 'Shout for someone, or call on speaker while pressing' is built from the St John Ambulance primary survey (shout for a helper, or put the phone on speaker and call yourself) and is not stated for bleeding alone.
- Seizure and a suspected neck or back injury: 'unless a neck or back injury is suspected' for turning onto the side and for the recovery position is built from the St John Ambulance spinal injury page (do not move unless in danger) combined with the seizure pages; no seizure page states it.
- A lone adult with no phone and a suspected neck or back injury: no opened source covers whether to shout for help, leave, or stay.
- Whether a family should leave written permission with a sitter or grandparent is general practice; no opened source states it. Needs clinician and legal review.
- Scope question, not a verification gap: the 988 Lifeline home page does not mention children; the AAP page (source 3) says parents and caregivers can use 988 for a child or teen in crisis. Whether it belongs in a guide for ages 1 to puberty is a clinician question.
- The AAP anaphylaxis page allows a prescribed inhaler or antihistamine in addition to epinephrine; this guide omits that and says never in place of epinephrine (the more cautious reading).
- Consent: the AAP article (source 8) is written for paediatric practices and concerns non-urgent care brought by someone other than a legal guardian; it does not set out emergency consent rules.
