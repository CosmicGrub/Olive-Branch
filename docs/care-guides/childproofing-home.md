# Baby-proofing and child-proofing a home, by room and by age

> **About the ages labels.** The 'ages' labels are scoping words. A label is source-backed only where a source states an age (for example gates until at least 2 years [10], laundry packets and children 6 and under [23, 25], crib to toddler bed at about 35 inches [20], no helmet or bike travel under 1 year [16], back seat to 13 [7], recommendations for babies up to 1 year [0]). Other labels are generic stage words (babies, crawling and walking children, young children) and not numeric claims. Clinician to confirm before any age-gating in the app.

## Top hazards

- **Unsafe sleep** (0-12m): Back to sleep, firm flat level surface (never inclined), bare sleep space, never on an adult bed, sofa or armchair, room share without bed sharing. [0] [1] [11] [31] [32]
- **Drowning** (leading cause of death at 1-4y; second leading cause of unintentional injury death at 5-14y): Arm's reach, four-sided 4 ft fence with self-latching gate, never alone in the bath, life jackets. [5] [10] [20]
- **Firearms** (all ages): Safest is no gun; otherwise locked, unloaded, ammunition locked separately. [3] [4] [29]
- **Poisoning (medicines, cleaners, laundry packets, nicotine, cannabis, alcohol)** (young children mainly): Lock up, original containers, call Poison Help 1-800-222-1222, do not induce vomiting. [3] [13] [23] [25]
- **Button batteries and magnets** (young children, and teens for magnets): Out of reach; if you suspect a child swallowed a battery, or one is in the nose, ear or throat, go to emergency care or call 911 straight away; if a magnet may have been swallowed or has hurt a child, call your pediatrician or go to the nearest emergency department straight away. [9] [25] [34]
- **Falls (stairs, windows, furniture, carriers)** (babies and young children): Gates, window stops (no more than 4 in; the UK NHS says under 6.5 cm / 2.5 in, so aim for the smaller gap), carriers on floor, harnesses. [2] [10] [26]
- **Furniture and TV tip-over** (young children): Anchor to wall or floor. [2] [6] [26]
- **Choking** (babies and young children): Cut food to half-inch or smaller, round firm foods only fully chopped, supervise meals, keep small parts and balloons away. [10] [18]
- **Strangulation (blind cords, drawstrings, cords)** (babies and young children): Cordless blinds, secure cords, no pacifier cords, no drawstrings on playgrounds. [2] [10] [17]
- **Burns and scalds** (all young children): Water heater at or below 120F (49C), hot drinks out of reach, screens on heaters. [2] [10] [20]
- **Vehicle heatstroke** (children under 15): Never leave a child in a car; look before you lock. [7] [22]
- **Fire and carbon monoxide** (all ages): Working smoke alarms on every level and in bedrooms; CO alarms near sleeping areas. [2] [8] [12]

## By room

### Nursery / sleep space

- [ ] Put baby on their back for every sleep, naps and nights, including babies with reflux. Side sleeping is as dangerous as stomach sleeping. *(ages: 0-12m. Why: Lowers SIDS and sleep-related death risk.)* [0] [1] [31] [32]
- [ ] Use a firm, flat, level sleep surface that is not tilted at all: a crib, a bassinet, a play yard or a portable crib made to safety standards, with only a snug fitted sheet. *(ages: 0-12m. Why: Soft, tilted or raised surfaces can block a baby's breathing (positional asphyxia). The CDC, NICHD and Health Canada all call for a flat, level surface with no slope, so no slope at all is the safe choice.)* [0] [1] [11] [32]
- [ ] Do not use inclined sleepers, hammocks, crib wedges, nursing pillows, loungers or baby nests for sleep, and do not use any product for sleep that is not made for infant sleep. Any alternative infant sleep product must meet the federal (CPSC) infant sleep standards. *(ages: 0-12m. Why: Products that tilt or cradle a baby can let the head slump or the body slide into a position where breathing is blocked.)* [0] [2] [32]
- [ ] Keep pillows, blankets, quilts, bumper pads, positioners, soft toys and weighted items out of the sleep space. Dress baby in one more layer than an adult would wear, or use a wearable blanket. Do not put a hat on baby indoors or cover their head. *(ages: 0-12m. Why: Suffocation, entrapment and overheating.)* [0] [1] [11] [31] [32]
- [ ] Keep the baby's sleep space in your room, near but not in your bed, for at least the first 6 months. *(ages: first 6 months or more. Why: Room sharing is linked to as much as 50% lower SIDS risk (AAP, NICHD).)* [0] [1] [11] [31] [32]
- [ ] Do not bed share. The AAP says to avoid it under any circumstances; room sharing without bed sharing is safest. Never fall asleep with baby on a sofa or armchair. *(ages: 0-12m. Why: AAP: the risk of sleep-related death is 5 to 10 times higher when sharing a bed under 4 months, more than 10 times higher after alcohol, drugs or sedating medicine, and up to 67 times higher on a couch, soft armchair or cushion.)* [0] [11] [32]
- [ ] Never let baby sleep on an adult bed, couch, sofa, armchair, waterbed, air mattress or futon, whether alone or with someone else. If baby has dropped off on one of these, move them to their own flat sleep space. *(ages: 0-12m. Why: Soft adult surfaces make SIDS and other sleep-related deaths more likely, and a baby can be trapped or smothered; couches and armchairs are especially dangerous if an adult dozes off on them.)* [0] [11] [32]
- [ ] If baby nods off in a car seat, stroller, swing, carrier or sling, lay them on their back on a firm, flat surface as soon as you can. *(ages: 0-12m. Why: Slumped upright sleep can block breathing.)* [0] [32]
- [ ] If you use a sling or carrier, keep baby's face visible at all times, keep them close and upright with the chin off the chest, and do not feed baby in it (take them out first). Do not zip a coat around baby in a carrier. *(ages: babies. Why: Suffocation and overheating.)* [10] [32]
- [ ] Swaddling is optional and does not lower SIDS risk. If you swaddle: always on the back, not too tight, and stop as soon as baby looks like they are trying to roll (often 3-4 months, sometimes earlier). Never use weighted swaddles. *(ages: until baby shows signs of rolling. Why: The risk of suffocation is higher if a swaddled baby rolls onto their stomach (AAP).)* [0]
- [ ] Offer a pacifier for naps and at bedtime (if breastfeeding, wait until it is well established). Do not hang a pacifier on a cord or clip it to clothing. *(ages: 0-12m. Why: Pacifiers lower SIDS risk (AAP, CDC). A cord or clip can strangle a baby. The AAP bars neck cords and clothing clips during sleep and the NHS allows a clip only while an adult is watching, so using neither is the safest rule.)* [0] [1] [10]
- [ ] Check cribs and used cribs for recalls, missing hardware and instructions; do not use a broken crib. Lower the mattress as baby grows; consider a toddler bed at about 35 inches tall or when the child starts climbing out. *(ages: until climbing out or about 35 in. Why: Falls out of cribs and crib failure.)* [0] [20]
- [ ] Keep the room smoke-free; no smoking or vaping around baby. Room about 16-20C (UK guidance); check for overheating (sweating, hot chest or flushed skin). *(ages: 0-12m. Why: Smoke exposure and overheating raise SIDS risk.)* [0] [1] [31]
- [ ] Do not use baby walkers. Use a stationary activity center, play yard or high chair instead. *(ages: infants (no age range is given by the sources). Why: Walkers can roll down stairs, let a child reach hot or poisonous things, and the AAP says they can delay when a child starts to walk.)* [19] [26]

### Living room / family room

- [ ] Anchor TVs, TV stands, bookcases, dressers, shelves, desks and chests to the wall or floor. *(ages: young children. Why: Young children climb on furniture and TVs, lean on them and pull themselves up, and the furniture can tip over and crush a child.)* [2] [3] [6] [26]
- [ ] Pad or remove sharp-cornered furniture (coffee tables are a particular hazard) from walking areas; put corner and edge bumpers on furniture and hearths. *(ages: when learning to walk. Why: Head and face injuries from falls against sharp edges.)* [2] [3] [10]
- [ ] Screen fireplaces, wood stoves, space heaters, hot radiators and hot vents. Fireguards should enclose the whole fire or heater and be fixed to the wall; barriers should have the lowest point of their top edge at least 56 cm (22 in) off the floor. *(ages: young children. Why: Contact burns.)* [3] [10] [20]
- [ ] Cover unused outlets with plugs too big to be a choking hazard; use tamper-resistant receptacles when replacing; keep cords out of reach. *(ages: young children. Why: Electrical injury and cord pulling.)* [2] [3] [20]
- [ ] Check floors at a child's eye level for coins, beads, buttons, pins, pills and small toy parts; keep older children's small toys separate from babies and toddlers. *(ages: babies and toddlers. Why: Choking and ingestion.)* [3] [10] [18]
- [ ] Know which devices use button or lithium coin batteries (remotes, key fobs, thermometers, flameless candles, musical cards, hearing aids) and keep them out of reach; tape battery compartments shut. Batteries can also lodge in the nose or ear. Post the National Battery Ingestion Hotline number (800-498-8666) with your other emergency numbers. *(ages: all young children. Why: A lithium coin battery stuck in the oesophagus can burn through it within 2 hours. If you suspect a child has swallowed one, or a battery is in the nose, ear or throat, go to an emergency department or call 911 straight away; serious damage can happen quickly.)* [3] [9] [25]
- [ ] Keep loose magnets, especially small high-powered ones, and magnetic toys with small pieces away from children. The AAP urges families with children not to keep high-powered magnet sets at home and to get rid of any rare-earth magnets; otherwise keep small or loose magnets in a locked container up high. Check magnetic toys often for cracks. *(ages: babies, toddlers and older children (including teens). Why: If more than one high-powered magnet is swallowed they can pull together inside the body and cause serious damage. Symptoms can include stomach pain, vomiting and fever, which are common in children, so a swallowed magnet may not be obvious. If a magnet may have been swallowed, or a child has been hurt by one, get in touch with your pediatrician or go to the nearest emergency department straight away; waiting can lead to severe injury.)* [10] [33] [34]
- [ ] Know the plants in your home and yard; consider removing poisonous ones or keeping them out of reach. Poison Help can tell you which plants are harmful. *(ages: young children and pets. Why: Poisoning.)* [3] [20] [25]

### Stairs and doors

- [ ] Fit a gate at both the top and the bottom of the stairs once baby starts to crawl and walk. At the top, use a gate that screws to the wall or studs, not a pressure-mounted or accordion-style gate; check the gate is rated for top-of-stairs use. Do not use pet gates. *(ages: crawling and walking children. Why: Falls; accordion and V-shaped gates can trap a head or neck.)* [2] [3] [20] [26]
- [ ] Keep stair gates in place until the child is at least 2 (UK guidance); close the gate every time. *(ages: to at least 2y (NHS). Why: Falls continue as children learn to climb.)* [10]
- [ ] Hold the handrail when carrying a baby; keep stairs clear of toys and clutter and well lit. If stairs are carpeted, tack the carpet down firmly at the edges. Consider anti-slip rugs, and do not let a child run around in socks on hardwood floors. *(ages: all ages. Why: Adult falls while carrying a baby, and slips.)* [3] [10] [26]
- [ ] Use door-knob covers or locks on rooms you do not want children to enter (covers must still let an adult in quickly in an emergency). Fasten glass doors open; remove swinging and folding doors until the child is old enough to grasp how they operate. *(ages: young children. Why: Access to hazardous rooms and finger and hand injuries.)* [2] [3] [20]
- [ ] Cover banister or balcony gaps wider than 6.5 cm (2.5 in) with boards or safety netting (UK NHS guidance). *(ages: babies and toddlers. Why: Head and body entrapment and falls.)* [10]

### Windows (every room)

- [ ] Fit window guards or stops so windows open only a small gap (screens do not stop falls). Aim for the smallest gap: US and Canadian pages say no more than 4 inches (10 cm), and the UK NHS says under 6.5 cm (2.5 in), so the NHS figure is the safer target. Guards must still be openable by an adult or older child in an emergency, so keep at least one usable escape window in each room, and make sure adults know where any window keys are kept. *(ages: from when a child can crawl and climb. Why: Falls from windows. The 4 in (10 cm) and 6.5 cm (2.5 in) limits come from different countries; the smaller gap is the safer choice.)* [2] [10] [20] [26]
- [ ] Open double-hung windows from the top, keep them locked and closed when not in use, and move cribs, chairs, sofas, low tables and other climbable furniture away from windows. *(ages: crawlers and climbers. Why: Children climb furniture to reach windows.)* [3] [10] [26]
- [ ] Prefer cordless blinds and shades. If you keep corded ones: anchor cords with floor mounts or wall brackets, add stop devices, cut looped cords and add safety tassels, shorten pull cords, and keep cribs and beds away from cords. *(ages: babies and young children. Why: Strangulation.)* [2] [3] [10] [20]

### Kitchen

- [ ] Lock cabinets and drawers holding cleaners, medicines, matches, lighters and knives; keep products locked even if the packaging is child-resistant. *(ages: young children. Why: Poisoning, cuts. Child-resistant packaging slows children down but is not child-proof.)* [2] [10] [25]
- [ ] Use the back burners and turn pot handles inward or to the back. Keep hot drinks well out of reach (a drink can still scald a child a quarter of an hour after it was made), put a hot drink down well out of reach before holding a baby, and use cups with tight-fitting lids. *(ages: babies and young children. Why: Scalds.)* [10] [20]
- [ ] Install anti-tip brackets on freestanding ranges and stoves. Choose a cordless kettle, or one whose flex is short or coiled. *(ages: young children. Why: Tip-over and cord pulling.)* [2] [10]
- [ ] Warm milk in a bottle warmer or a jug or pot of hot water, not the microwave (hot spots); test a few drops on your wrist before feeding. *(ages: babies. Why: Scalds from uneven heating.)* [10] [20]
- [ ] Use a harness (the NHS describes a 5-point harness) in high chairs, strollers and prams every time; set carriers and bouncers on the floor, never on counters or tables. *(ages: babies and toddlers. Why: Falls.)* [10] [26]
- [ ] Child-resistant lids or out-of-reach bins for trash with spoiled food, razor blades and batteries. Knot plastic bags (including dry-cleaning bags) before discarding. *(ages: babies and young children. Why: Ingestion and suffocation.)* [3]
- [ ] Cut up food for babies and young children so that no piece is bigger than half an inch, and teach them to chew well. Keep high-risk foods (hot dogs, whole grapes, raw carrot sticks, nuts and seeds, chewing gum, hard or sticky sweets) from children until about age 4 or older depending on development; round firm foods may be served only if completely chopped into tiny pieces. Children sit to eat; supervise meals. *(ages: from starting solids; high-risk foods to about 4y. Why: NHS: food is the most common thing babies and toddlers choke on. AAP: small round objects (balloons, balls, marbles, toy pieces and round foods) cause the most choking deaths.)* [10] [18]

### Bathroom

- [ ] Never leave a baby or young child alone in the bath, even for a moment, even if an older brother or sister is in the bath with them. Bath seats and rings are not safety devices. Empty the tub right after use. *(ages: babies and young children. Why: Drowning can happen in seconds and silently, in very shallow water.)* [5] [10] [20]
- [ ] Set the water heater to 120F (49C) or lower, or fit anti-scald devices on faucets and shower heads. At bath time run cold water in first, then add hot; check the temperature with your elbow or wrist before the child gets in; bathe the child away from the faucets; take them out of the tub before running more hot water. *(ages: all ages. Why: Scalds.)* [2] [10] [20]
- [ ] Use non-slip mats or decals in the tub and shower. *(ages: children. Why: Slips.)* [26]
- [ ] Lock up or keep out of reach all medicines, including any in purses and bags. *(ages: all young children. Why: Poisoning. The NHS lists medicines first among causes of hospital admission for poisoning in under-5s.)* [3] [10] [20] [25]

### Laundry room / utility

- [ ] While children 6 and under live in or visit the home, use traditional liquid or powder detergent rather than single-use packets. If you keep packets, keep them in the original container, locked and high up, not on or under the washer or dryer. *(ages: children 6 and under. Why: Packets are concentrated, look like candy and can cause serious breathing, stomach and eye injuries.)* [23] [25]
- [ ] Never mix detergent with ammonia or other cleaning products (some mixtures release dangerous fumes); keep the laundry room door shut and locked whenever you are not in there. *(ages: all ages. Why: Toxic fumes and curious children getting in.)* [23]
- [ ] Keep chemicals and cleaning products in their original containers; never transfer them into another container, especially a food or drink container. *(ages: all ages. Why: Mistaken ingestion.)* [21] [25]
- [ ] UK: keep liquid laundry capsules, dishwasher tablets and toilet gel discs out of sight and reach, and pick cleaning products with a bittering agent added (it makes them taste unpleasant). *(ages: babies and young children. Why: Poisoning and chemical burns.)* [10]

### Bedrooms / older children's rooms

- [ ] Keep medicines locked (a locked toolbox works). If anyone in the home takes opioids, lock them away and have naloxone on hand; dispose of unused medicines. *(ages: all ages. Why: Overdose and self-harm risk. AAP: every child can be at risk.)* [28]
- [ ] Lock up alcohol and empty unfinished drinks straight away. *(ages: all ages. Why: Alcohol is very toxic to a young child; alcohol use can increase impulsive harm in young people.)* [3] [28]
- [ ] Keep liquid nicotine (vaping e-liquid) and cannabis edibles locked or out of reach, in original child-resistant packaging, apart from normal food. *(ages: all young children. Why: Even a small amount of liquid nicotine can be fatal; edibles can look like sweets.)* [3] [13] [25]
- [ ] If a child or teen may be thinking about suicide, put knives, razors, belts, ropes and similar items somewhere secure, and be ready to take the locks off bathroom and bedroom doors (the AAP says this may be necessary). Call or text 988 (US). Talking about suicide does not make it more likely to happen. If a child talks about hurting themselves, get help right away: phone their doctor or go to an emergency department. *(ages: any child or teen. Why: Reduces access to lethal means during a crisis. The AAP says pediatricians now look for depression and suicide risk in every child at checkups from age 12.)* [28] [36]

### Firearms (wherever they are)

- [ ] The safest home for a child has no gun; remove firearms from the home if you can. If there is one: store it unloaded in a locked case or safe, ammunition locked separately, and keep keys and combinations away from children and teens. *(ages: all ages. Why: Even toddlers are strong enough to pull a trigger; teen risk includes impulsive self-harm.)* [3] [4] [28] [29]
- [ ] Do not rely on hiding the gun or on teaching children not to touch it. *(ages: all ages. Why: Children are curious and kids have found guns their parents thought were well hidden; many studies show teaching alone is not enough.)* [4] [29]
- [ ] Do not leave a loaded or unlocked gun in a car or anywhere on the property. *(ages: all ages. Why: Access outside the home.)* [29]

### Water: pools, ponds, buckets

- [ ] Stay within arm's reach of a young child in or near water, with no phone or alcohol distractions, even if a lifeguard is present or the child has had swimming lessons. *(ages: young children. Why: Drowning is the top cause of death for ages 1-4 and the second most common cause of accidental injury death for ages 5-14 (CDC).)* [5]
- [ ] Home pools: a four-sided fence, at least 4 feet tall, enclosing the pool completely and keeping it apart from the house, with a self-closing, self-latching gate. Add layers such as self-closing, self-locking doors or door alarms, pool alarms and a power safety cover, and re-establish the barrier after every use. When nobody is using the pool, take out any toys that could draw a child in. *(ages: all ages. Why: Barriers cut unsupervised access.)* [2] [5]
- [ ] If a child is missing, check the pool first. *(ages: all ages. Why: Seconds matter.)* [2] [22]
- [ ] Fence, fill in or securely cover garden ponds; empty and store paddling pools straight after use. Drowning can also happen in bathtubs and water-filled buckets, so stay with a child near any standing water. *(ages: babies and toddlers. Why: Children can drown in a few centimetres of water.)* [5] [10] [20]
- [ ] Use a properly fitted US Coast Guard-approved life jacket on boats and around open water. Floaties, arm bands and water wings are not safety devices. *(ages: all ages. Why: Inflatables slip off and create false security.)* [5]

### Car and driveway

- [ ] Never leave a child alone in a car, even briefly; a cracked window, shade or air conditioning does not make it safe. Look in the back seat every time, put your phone or bag in the back, and ask your caregiver to call if the child is more than 10 minutes late. *(ages: all children (heatstroke is the biggest non-crash vehicle killer of children under 15). Why: A car can warm about 20F in 10 minutes; a child overheats 3 to 5 times as fast as an adult; heatstroke can occur when it is as cool as 57F outside.)* [7] [22]
- [ ] Keep cars locked when parked, keys out of children's reach, and rear fold-down seats closed. Teach children that cars and trunks are not play areas. *(ages: young children. Why: Children who climb into cars can lock themselves in (AAP: 1 in 4 hot car deaths).)* [22]
- [ ] If you notice a child alone in a car and are worried, call 911 straight away. If the child is unresponsive or in pain: call 911, get them out of the car and spray them with cool water (never an ice bath). If the child is responding to you, remain with them until help comes. *(ages: all ages. Why: Emergency response.)* [22]
- [ ] Use rear-facing as long as the seat allows, then a forward-facing harness seat as long as possible (at least to age 4), then a belt-positioning booster until the seat belt fits (about 4 ft 9 in tall, typically 8 to 12 years), then the seat belt; children under 13 ride in the back seat. *(ages: 0-12y. Why: Crash protection by size.)* [7]
- [ ] Never run a car or truck in a garage attached to the house, even with the garage door open. Run generators only outdoors, over 20 feet from any window, door or vent; never inside the home or garage, even with doors and windows open. *(ages: all ages. Why: Carbon monoxide.)* [8]

### Fire and carbon monoxide (whole home)

- [ ] Smoke alarms on every level including the basement, inside each bedroom and outside sleeping areas; interconnected if possible; test monthly; replace the whole unit every 10 years; change non-sealed batteries at least yearly. *(ages: all ages. Why: Early warning.)* [2] [3] [12]
- [ ] Carbon monoxide alarms outside bedrooms near every sleeping area, battery-operated or with battery backup; replace per the manufacturer or every 5 years; check batteries when clocks change. *(ages: all ages. Why: CO is a poison gas you cannot see or smell.)* [2] [8]
- [ ] Practise a fire escape plan; tell sitters to get out at once if there is a fire, then call the fire department from a mobile phone or a neighbor's home. *(ages: all ages. Why: Children cannot plan an escape alone.)* [3] [24]

### Outdoors, playground, bikes

- [ ] Playground surface: a thick layer (12 or more inches) of wood chips, mulch, sand or pea gravel, or safety-tested rubber mats, reaching 6 feet or more out on every side; not concrete, asphalt or grass. Platforms higher than 30 inches that are meant for school-aged children need guardrails or barriers. Supervise actively. *(ages: children on playgrounds. Why: More than 200,000 children a year are treated in emergency departments for playground injuries (CPSC figure quoted by AAP); most injuries are falls, so the surface under the equipment matters.)* [17]
- [ ] Avoid drawstring sweatshirts and necklaces on the playground, and keep jump ropes and pet leashes off the equipment; take off helmets before playing on equipment; choose shade, as metal and plastic slides get hot enough to burn. *(ages: children on playgrounds. Why: Strangulation and burns.)* [17]
- [ ] Bicycle helmet that meets the CPSC standard, sitting level on the head with the top of the forehead covered. The child should be able to see the helmet brim when looking up, and the chin strap should be snug. Replace after a serious crash and about every 5 years. Neither helmets nor bike travel are recommended for babies under 1. *(ages: children who ride bikes. Why: Head injury.)* [16]
- [ ] The AAP advises against home trampolines altogether; if one is used, limit jumpers to one at a time. *(ages: all ages. Why: Thousands of injuries a year, especially under age 6.)* [27]
- [ ] Before a child plays at another home, ask about supervision, pools (4-foot four-sided fence with self-latching gate), trampolines, firearms (unloaded, locked, ammunition separate), screens and pets. For sleepovers, ask who will be in the home; a 'lateover' (home at bedtime) is an option. *(ages: when visiting other homes. Why: Risks outside your control.)* [27] [4]

## By age

### 0-3m

- [ ] Back to sleep on a firm, flat, level surface in your room (not your bed, and never an adult bed, sofa or armchair); nothing soft in the sleep space; smoke-free home. [0] [1] [11] [31] [32]
- [ ] Do not bed share; the AAP reports sleep-related death is 5 to 10 times more likely when a baby under 4 months shares a bed. [0]
- [ ] Never leave baby alone on a bed, sofa or changing table; keep carriers and bouncers on the floor. [10] [26]
- [ ] Install the car seat rear-facing before the first ride home; never leave baby in a car. [7] [22]
- [ ] Check bath temperature with your elbow or wrist and stay with baby throughout; never leave baby alone in water. [10] [20]
- [ ] Set the water heater at 120F (49C) or lower; keep hot drinks away from baby. [2] [10] [20]
- [ ] Smoke and CO alarms working before baby comes home. [2] [8]
- [ ] If you use a sling or carrier, keep baby's face visible and do not feed in it. [10] [32]

### 3-12m

- [ ] Stop swaddling at the first signs of trying to roll; move to a wearable blanket. [0] [32]
- [ ] Keep room sharing to at least 6 months; keep the crib clear even as baby rolls. [0] [31]
- [ ] Gates at stairs once crawling; anchor furniture; cover outlets; secure blind and other cords. [2] [3] [10]
- [ ] Check floors at eye level for small objects such as coins, buttons and small toy parts. [3] [18]
- [ ] Keep coin and button batteries and loose magnets out of reach. [9] [10] [34]
- [ ] Lock cleaners and medicines; keep purses up; no walkers. [19] [25]
- [ ] Harness in high chair and stroller; carriers on the floor. [10] [26]
- [ ] Cut foods into pieces no larger than half an inch; avoid high-risk choking foods. [18]

## Where sources disagree

Clinician-facing section. These records show where the cited pages differ; nothing a parent needs is held only here, because every parent-facing item above states its own rule in full.

### Bed sharing

- Avoid bed sharing under any circumstances. [0]
- Bed sharing is not recommended because it raises the risk of SIDS and sleep-related death; sleeping in the same room but on separate surfaces is the lower-risk choice. [11]
- Says bed sharing increases SIDS risk and other dangers, and lists safer-set-up steps for anyone who bed shares even occasionally. [32]
- Advises avoiding it in higher-risk cases (premature, low birth weight, smoker, alcohol/drugs/sedating medicine) and gives steps if sharing; never on a sofa. [31]
- The CDC page opened does not address bed sharing. [1]

**Carried forward / note:** Carry the most cautious reading: do not bed share (AAP: avoid under any circumstances), never on a sofa or armchair. The app must not teach 'how to bed-share'; the harm-reduction steps in sources 0, 31 and 32 are deliberately NOT carried into the advice lists. Flag for clinician.

### Maximum window opening

- Windows and guard bars should leave a gap of no more than four inches; keep one escape window per room. [2]
- Latch windows so they cannot open more than 10 cm (4 in). [20]
- Window stops so windows open no more than four inches. [26]
- UK: fit locks or catches that keep the gap under 6.5 cm (2.5 in). [10]

**Carried forward / note:** US and Canada say 4 in (10 cm); UK says under 6.5 cm (2.5 in). Carry the smaller figure (6.5 cm / about 2.5 in) as the target, show both, and keep the adult escape route (at least one openable escape window per room). Clinician/safety reviewer to confirm.

### Sleep surface incline

- Choose a firm, flat surface; one tilted beyond 10 degrees is unsafe. Elsewhere on the page a newborn's bassinet is described as having 'no incline'. [0]
- The sleep surface should be firm and flat, with no angle or incline. [1]
- The sleep surface should be firm, flat and level; one with a raised end is unsafe because baby can slide and the head can slump. [11]
- Sleep surfaces must be flat; inclined sleepers, baby hammocks and crib wedges are named as unsafe. [32]

**Carried forward / note:** Not every source gives the same threshold (AAP names 10 degrees as the unsafe limit; CDC, NICHD and Health Canada say not inclined). Carry the most cautious reading: flat and level, no incline at all. Do not tell parents that up to 10 degrees is fine.

### Stair gates: how long, and which type at the top

- Gates at top and bottom once crawling or walking; wall-stud mounted, not accordion style. [3]
- Gates held in place by pressure should not be used at the head of stairs; use gates fixed into studs. [20]
- Confirm the gate is rated for top-of-stairs use; attach to the wall when possible. [26]
- At the head of the stairs, choose a gate that is screwed to the wall. [2]
- Keep gates until child is at least 2. [10]

**Carried forward / note:** Sources agree on hardware-mounted at the top. Only NHS gives a minimum age. Carry hardware mount at top and no end age before 2.

### Depth of water that is dangerous

- Babies can drown in as little as 5 cm (2 in) of water. [10]
- Water only 2.5 cm (1 inch) deep can be enough for a child to drown. [20]

**Carried forward / note:** Carry the smaller figure: any amount of standing water is a risk. Avoid quoting a safe depth.

### Swaddling

- Swaddling does not lower SIDS risk; stop once baby shows signs of rolling (often 3-4 months); never use weighted swaddles. [0]
- Advises against swaddling when bed-sharing; baby sleeping bags are the safer option. [31]
- Suggests sleep sacks instead of loose blankets; swaddling not discussed. [32]

**Carried forward / note:** No source says swaddling is required. Carry: optional, back-sleeping only, stop at first rolling signs; wearable blanket preferred. Flag for clinician.

### Pacifier clips and cords

- Do not hang a pacifier from the neck or fasten it to clothes while baby sleeps. [0]
- UK: no tying or clipping a dummy to clothes; a clip meeting BS EN 12586 only while baby is being watched. [10]
- Recommends a short ribbon clip for the pacifier, and says not to hang it around the neck. [20]

**Carried forward / note:** Sources differ. Carry the most cautious reading: no cord and no clip on clothing; a clip is allowed only in the UK source and only while watching. Flag for clinician.

### Crib bumpers and positioners

- Names bumper pads, and similar items fixed to the crib slats, among soft items that raise the risk of entrapment, suffocation or strangulation. [0]
- Bumpers raise suffocation and entrapment risk; positioners grouped with items to keep out. [11]
- Avoid cot bumpers (choking, suffocation, strangulation). [10]
- Remove bumper pads and positioners from the sleep space. [32]

**Carried forward / note:** No disagreement among opened sources; recorded because older advice sold bumpers as protective. Positioners are named on the NICHD and Health Canada pages, not on the AAP page. Carry: no bumpers or positioners.

### Baby walkers

- AAP calls for banning wheeled walkers and says to throw them out; walkers can delay when a child starts to walk; Canada has banned them; stationary activity centres are an alternative. [19]
- Skip baby walkers; use a stable stationary activity center. [26]

**Carried forward / note:** Consistent. The AAP page says rules in place since 1997 have not removed the risks and calls for a ban on walkers with wheels; the most cautious reading is no walkers.

### Gun storage wording

- Safest home has no gun; if you keep one, locked, unloaded, ammunition locked separately. [29]
- Store every firearm locked and unloaded, ammunition separate. [4]
- Remove firearms from the home if you can. [28]

**Carried forward / note:** Consistent. The app should present 'no gun' as safest and storage as the minimum if kept.

### Honey after swallowing a button battery (clinician-facing only; not for display to parents)

- Go to the emergency room, or phone 911, first. The page also describes a honey step for children over 12 months old; the details are not carried here. [9]
- Go to the nearest emergency department immediately. This page also mentions honey on the way and does not apply the AAP's over-12-months limit. [20]

**Carried forward / note:** CLINICIAN-FACING RECORD ONLY, not advice for parents, and not to be shown in the app. The two pages differ on honey and on age. No dose or timing is recorded in this guide. The only parent-facing instruction is: go to the emergency department or call 911 now, and call the battery ingestion hotline, 800-498-8666. Do not add honey text without clinician sign-off.
