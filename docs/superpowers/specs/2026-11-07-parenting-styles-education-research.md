## 2. Where the sources agree (high confidence)

1. **Two dimensions, not four boxes.** Warmth/responsiveness and expectations/demandingness. The four styles are corners of a plane. Real parents move around in it by day, child and situation.
2. **Warmth plus clear expectations is the best-supported combination** across the documents and the online sources, for broad outcomes in Western samples.
3. **Neglect/uninvolvement is the worst-supported position**, and the Child Find sheet and the research agree.
4. **Explaining the rule beats just enforcing it.** Every document that discusses discipline says explain, be specific, stay calm.
5. **Consistency and follow-through matter** (time-out checklists, natural and logical consequences).
6. **Physical punishment does not work well.** AAP calls for it to be avoided. The scanned handout's "authoritarian = hitting" line matches this. The Child Find sheet says the same.

## 3. Where the sources disagree, or need correction

| Claim in the PDFs | Cross-check | How the app should treat it |
|---|---|---|
| "Authoritative: risks, none." (Child Find) | Overclaim. Evidence is correlational and effects are modest. | Never present any style as risk-free or guaranteed. |
| Style *causes* outcomes | Twin and adoption designs show a real share of the link is child-driven (genes and temperament). A 2014 meta-analysis put heritability of parenting at about 23%. Bidirectional. | Say "is linked to", never "produces". Do not blame parents or children. |
| Authoritarian always harms | Chao 1994: the same style predicted school success among Asian American children and the reverse among European Americans. Meaning differs by culture. | Teach style as *climate*, not a verdict. Offer a "what this means in your family's culture" note. Do not label families. |
| Style predicts outcomes by itself | Darling and Steinberg: style is a *context* that changes how specific practices land. | Teach practices (what you do) and climate (how it feels to the child) separately. |
| "Chairing": face the wall, no crying allowed, 30 minutes, send to room, "never reason with a child", "use a firm, loud voice", early bedtime at 6 pm as discipline | AAP: time-out is about one minute per year of age, calm, then reconnect. Isolation until the child "says the right reason" and punishing crying run against emotion-coaching evidence. Sending to bed as a punishment can harm sleep routines. | Do **not** build chairing. Offer AAP-aligned time-out and "time-in" only, flagged as guidance, not a rule. |
| Feel-good praise cues ("Good boy for...") | Specific, effortful praise is the better-supported form (the same handout's own tip 5 says "honest, specific praise"). | Offer specific-praise prompts only. |
| "These kids enjoy making you mad" (disruptive-behaviour page) | Not evidence-based. Attributing motive to a child can escalate conflict. | Omit. |
| "Don't ask a child if they did what you saw" (tip) | Reasonable, and consistent with avoiding set-ups to lie. | Keep. |

## 4. Evidence strength, plainly

- **Strong:** neglect harms; physical punishment is ineffective and risks harm; warmth and structure together are the best-supported pairing in the research most of these sources draw from.
- **Moderate:** consistency between caregivers helps (Bielefeld longitudinal; a Spanish study of 9 to 11 year olds; a Singapore government study of cooperative co-parenting). It is not unanimous: an NDSU thesis found no effect of differing practices. **Conflict between caregivers appears to matter more than style differences.**
- **Weak or mixed:** emotion coaching. It reliably changes *parent behaviour* in trials (Tuning in to Kids, emotion-enhanced Triple P), but evidence that it improves children's emotion knowledge or behaviour at ages 5 to 7 is thin and mostly parent-reported or from small pilots. Most outcome claims in parent-facing material are not from controlled trials.
- **Unknown to us:** I did not find a reliable causal effect size for parenting style on outcomes once genetics are controlled. Say so.

## 5. What this means for Olive Branch

Olive Branch is child-first co-parenting with prohibitions P1 to P11. A parent-education feature must not breach them. In practice:

**Must not**
- Score, rank, label, or report on a parent. No "your style is permissive". No sharing a style result with the other guardian or the child. (A style quiz result is the parent's alone, stored on their own device or account, never visible to the co-parent.)
- Become evidence in a dispute. No exportable style history.
- Tell a parent that a style causes an outcome in their child.
- Appear in the child shell. The child shell has no settings and no adult content.

**Could (proposed, each needs the owner's yes)**
1. **Parent Learn tab** (guardian-only, in the parent app). Short, sourced cards: the two dimensions, the four corners, "style is climate; practices are tools", culture and temperament caveats, and an "evidence strength" chip on every claim (strong / moderate / weak / contested) with the source link.
2. **Private reflection, not a quiz.** "Where did I land today on warmth and expectations?" A two-slider check-in, stored privately, with a prompt rather than a grade.
3. **Situation playbooks for ages 5 to 7**, written to AAP-aligned practice: calm statement of the specific behaviour, brief time-out (about a minute per year), reconnect, specific praise, natural and logical consequences, plan-ahead rehearsal (taken from the useful parts of the scanned handout).
4. **Two-household bridge.** A shared, *optional* "house rules that match" checklist limited to core rules and routines (bedtime, screens, homework, safety), because consistency on those is the better-supported lever and style sameness is not required. Each guardian edits their own copy. Nothing is compared automatically.
5. **Repair cards.** What to do after you lost your temper. Models the "mistakes happen, then life goes on" message from the handout.

**Open decisions for the owner**
- Do you want a parent Learn tab at all, or keep this as reference material outside the app?
- Private reflection: store it, or keep it session-only?
- Which cultural framing do you want as the default, given that style labels do not travel across cultures?
- Is a matching-rules bridge wanted, or does it push toward comparing households?

## 6. Limits of this research

- Search excerpts were used, not full papers. Before quoting any statistic, open the original.
- The source PDFs are teaching handouts, so they simplify.
- The scanned PDF had several unreadable pages.
- This is education, not clinical advice. Parents worried about a child's behaviour should talk to their paediatrician.

## 7. Addendum: modern styles, communication and conflict (second research pass)

Search excerpts only, again. Evidence labels follow section 4.

### 7.1 "Modern" and "soft" parenting labels

| Label | What it is | What the evidence says | Label |
|---|---|---|---|
| Gentle / "soft" parenting | Warmth, validating feelings, firm-but-kind limits | No direct studies of the brand ([St. Thomas abstract](https://researchonline.stthomas.edu/esploro/outputs/journalArticle/Trying-to-remain-calmbut-I-do/991015340122403691)). Its ingredients match decades of authoritative-parenting research ([ParentData](https://parentdata.org/ask/is-gentle-parenting-evidence-based-does-it-actually-work)). The risk is drift into permissive when the boundary part is dropped. One study found parent uncertainty and burnout in over a third of its gentle-parent sample ([Rollins](https://scholarship.rollins.edu/as_facpub/345)). | Moderate (ingredients), weak (the brand) |
| Permissive / indulgent | Warm, few limits | Linked to lower self-discipline and responsibility; weaker links to BMI and sleep ([Parenting Science](https://www.parentingscience.com/permissive-parenting.html), advocacy site, treat with care). | Moderate |
| Helicopter / lawnmower | Hovering, or clearing every obstacle | College-student studies link more helicoptering to more depression and anxiety. Correlational ([Psychology Today](https://psychologytoday.com/ie/blog/behavior-problems-behavior-solutions/202106/the-trouble-helicopter-lawnmower-and-tiger-parents)). | Weak–moderate |
| Free-range | Age-appropriate independence | Mostly advocacy. Supporters cite autonomy benefits without sources. Not the same as neglect, and the legal line varies by place. | Weak |
| Attachment parenting | Close contact practices (babywearing, co-sleeping) | A set of infant-era practices, not a discipline style. Co-sleeping has separate safety guidance. Not covered by this pass. | Not assessed |
| Conscious parenting | Parent self-awareness first | Nothing found. | Not assessed |
| Positive Discipline (Nelsen) | Respectful, teaching-based discipline | Much less researched than behavioural parent-training programmes, early signs on attitudes ([PMC review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9560916)). | Weak |
| Time-in | Sit with the child to help them calm | Pilot data on feasibility only, no outcome data in what I found ([PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9149873)). Time-out has a strong behavioural track record. Its limitation is that it does not build connection. | Weak (time-in), strong (time-out works) |

**Takeaway.** "Gentle" and "authoritative" overlap heavily. The research line that matters is **limits held with warmth**, not the label. Do not teach any label as a brand to adopt.

### 7.2 Communication styles that parents are taught

- **Collaborative and Proactive Solutions (Ross Greene, "Plan B")**: empathy, define the problem, invite a joint solution, versus imposing (Plan A). Only secondary support found. Do not confuse it with the Think:Kids "Collaborative Problem Solving" programme ([Carlat](https://thecarlatreport.com/articles/1652-collaborative-problem-solving)). Weak–moderate, and name which model a claim refers to.
- **Nonviolent Communication** (feelings, needs, requests): nothing rigorous found for use with children.
- **Emotion coaching** and **specific praise**: see section 4.
- **Plain, specific instructions** (statements, not questions, from the handouts): consistent across the handouts and the AAP material. Strong enough to teach.

### 7.3 Conflict between the parents (the most important part)

- **How parents fight matters more than whether they do.** Destructive tactics (hostility, withdrawal) are linked to more internalising and externalising problems in children. The proposed mechanism is **emotional security**: a child who feels the parents' conflict threatens the family feels unsafe ([Notre Dame couples and kids](https://shaw.nd.edu/research/couples-and-kids)). Strong.
- **Constructive conflict** (calm, resolves, repairs where the child can see) is not harmful and can model problem-solving. Moderate.
- **Gottman's "four horsemen"** (criticism, contempt, defensiveness, stonewalling) describe destructive patterns. I found no study testing them on child outcomes directly. The closest is Katz and Gottman 1993. Teach them as a description, not a proven predictor.
- **Separated or divorced parents:** how parents handle conflict, living together or apart, is linked to children's emotional and behavioural problems (Harold and Sellers 2018 review, cited secondhand, [source](https://bwjp.org/?p=2310)). Strong–moderate.

### 7.4 When parents cannot communicate well (high conflict)

- **Parallel parenting** (minimal contact, written plan, clear schedules) is widely recommended for high conflict. Evidence that it improves outcomes is weak; the sources are law-firm and therapist blogs ([example](https://www.arizonalawgroup.com/blog/parallel-parenting-vs-co-parenting-which-approach-is-right-for-high-conflict-situations/)).
- **BIFF** (Brief, Informative, Friendly, Firm), a trademarked method for replying to hostile messages ([overview](https://stangelawfirm.com/articles/understanding-biff-responses-in-child-custody-matters-where-parents-have-to-co-parent-or-attempt-to-do-so/)). Practitioner method, not a trial-tested one. Useful rule: never use the child as a messenger.
- **Co-parenting apps** keep a timestamped record. That is useful, but also exactly why a private reflection must never be stored where it could be exported (see section 5).

### 7.5 What this adds to the Learn suite (proposals)

1. **"Limits with warmth" card** replacing any brand-name framing. Includes a "gentle but not permissive" check: *did I say the limit, and did I hold it?*
2. **Message check before sending** (guardian-to-guardian): a private, on-device helper. It flags tone patterns (blame, contempt, "you always"), suggests a brief, informative, friendly, firm rewrite, and never sends or stores anything by itself. It is a mirror, not a monitor. Needs owner approval because it touches the co-parent channel.
3. **"Keep the child out of the middle" card:** no messengers, no questioning about the other home, no criticising the other parent in earshot.
4. **Fight-in-front-of-the-child guide:** what constructive conflict looks like, and the repair ("we disagreed, we worked it out").
5. **Sibling conflict playbook for 5 to 7:** step in only when it is unsafe, name each child's feeling, ask what they could do, praise the fix. (Evidence for sibling-specific methods was not found in this pass, so label it Weak.)
6. **Evidence label on every one**, using the same four chips.

### 7.6 Gaps still open

- No full-text reading of any study.
- Sibling conflict at 5 to 7: no sibling-specific trials found.
- Nonviolent Communication with children: none found.
- Attachment and conscious parenting: not assessed here.
- Cultural and single-parent-household framing needs its own pass.
